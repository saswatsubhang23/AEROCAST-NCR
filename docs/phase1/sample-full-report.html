Background: the written reports

The reasoning, evidence and limits behind the figures above, as written in the application's Report page.

How the air of Delhi-NCR works, and how to read this application

Pollution in Delhi-NCR is not a single number. It is a field that the weather stirs, stacks up and sweeps away, and this application tries to show that field moving. This report explains the physical picture, what each page shows of it, and what it cannot yet see.

Four ideas that explain most of what you see

Pollutants differ in where they come from and how they behave. Fine particles (PM2.5) are small enough to travel hundreds of kilometres and to reach deep into the lungs; they come from combustion, from dust that has been ground fine, and from gases that turn into particles in the air. Coarse particles (PM10 minus PM2.5) are mostly dust and settle out within a day or so. Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) is a combustion gas, strongest beside traffic and power plants. Ozone (O₃) is not emitted at all: it is made in sunlight from other pollutants, and it is used up where fresh nitrogen oxides are plentiful, so it is often lowest in the most polluted places. Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) comes from burning coal and oil and slowly becomes sulphate particles.

The weather decides how much of an emission you breathe. Emissions change slowly; concentrations change fast, because the volume of air they are mixed into changes. At night the air near the ground is a shallow, still layer and the same traffic and the same fires produce high concentrations. By mid-afternoon the sun has heated the ground, the mixing layer can be a kilometre or two deep, and the same emissions are spread through ten times the volume. This is why Delhi's PM2.5 almost always peaks in the early hours and falls to its daily minimum around 16:00 to 17:00, and why the boundary-layer height is one of the most useful numbers in the whole application.

Wind moves pollution, and the region matters as much as the city. Delhi sits at the south-western end of the Indo-Gangetic plain, a broad, flat basin that ventilates poorly. In October and November the winds turn north-westerly and smoke from crop burning in Punjab and Haryana arrives on them; in winter, calm and cold air under a temperature inversion lets everything accumulate for days; in the monsoon, rain washes particles out of the air and winds come in from the south-east. Dust from Rajasthan and the Thar arrives in the pre-monsoon months. A station reading is therefore the sum of what is local and what has been carried in.

Chemistry connects the pollutants. Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides oxidise into sulphate and nitrate particles, so some of the PM2.5 you measure was a gas an hour or a day earlier. Ozone and nitrogen dioxide trade mass back and forth in sunlight. This is why the application can show a combined oxidant (Ox = O₃ + NO₂) that changes less than either does alone.

What each page shows

The Air Flow page is the main picture. Particles are carried along by the weather model's wind; each is coloured by the concentration of the air it is in, on the Central Pollution Control Board's index classes (green is good, dark red is severe). Underneath is a smooth concentration field. In 3D the same air moves at seven model heights from 10 m to about 3 km over real terrain. If you switch the source to Stored replay you see the last 72 hours of station observations; Live model shows the CAMS atmospheric model, which is the only source that is current; Coupled model carries a starting field forward with the weather and simple chemistry.

The Analysis tab is the workbench behind it: station observations hour by hour, the interpolation between stations with its measured error, weather-model fields, and simulations with adjustable wind and mixing. Early warning ranks every station by the trained model's forecast against a threshold and says how uncertain each forecast is. Weather and Air Quality hold the plain forecasts and the station tables. Data & Models says what data exists, what does not, and lets you train a new model; nothing trains unless you press the button.

Where pollution is likely to increase next

There are three ways the application answers this, and they are deliberately kept apart because they have different strengths.

A statistical forecast for each station. A model trained on a year of hourly observations and weather predicts PM2.5, PM10, NO₂ and O₃ at +1, +6 and +24 hours. On a held-out period it was closer than simply repeating the last value in eleven of twelve cases, and clearly better at six hours (PM2.5 error 15 against 18 µg/m³). It does not beat that baseline for PM10 a day ahead, and the application says so wherever that forecast appears. This is the most reliable answer to "what will this monitor read", but it needs fresh observations, and the station feed is currently days behind.

A physical picture of where the observed air goes. The transport and coupled models carry the current field with the wind and apply deposition, rain scavenging and sulphate formation. They show where polluted air is heading, which is exactly the question of regional movement. They have no emissions, so they cannot say where new pollution will appear, and on historical windows they did not beat simply holding the starting value. Read them as a picture of movement, not as a number to rely on.

An independent atmospheric model. CAMS (the Copernicus atmospheric composition service, reached through Open-Meteo) is a full chemistry model with emissions. It is updated every hour and covers the whole area, but its cells over Delhi are about 44 km across and, compared with the stations over the last two months, it was a worse predictor than "the same hour yesterday" (PM2.5 error 30 against 18.5 µg/m³; correlation 0.29). It is useful as regional context and as a check, not as a station forecast.

Who is exposed

Exposure is concentration multiplied by the number of people breathing it, weighted towards the sensitive: children, the elderly, people in hospitals and schools. The application does not yet compute this, because the two layers it needs are not loaded: a gridded population (WorldPop or GHSL are free) and a list of hospitals, schools and care homes. The database schema already has tables for both, and the Data Coverage page lists them as obtainable. Until they are loaded, any map that claimed to show "who is affected" would be inventing it, so none does.

How to read the colours and labels

Every layer carries a badge that says what kind of number it is. Observed is a measurement at a station. Spatial estimate is an interpolation between stations and is clear wherever fewer than two stations are within 12 km. Weather forecast and Atmospheric model are model output, not measurements. Model forecast is the trained model's prediction for one station. Simulated and Hypothetical are computed from assumptions, and scenarios are what-ifs rather than predictions. A Stale badge means the newest data are older than the limit; the application then describes the past and says so.

What is missing

The honest list of absent data is on the Data Coverage page and in the data-sources report. The ones that matter most for the questions above are an emissions inventory, fire detections, nitrogen oxide and station-weather observations (offered by a feed already in use but not yet stored), vertical profiles of pollutants, and the population and sensitive-site layers. Several can be added without cost; the reports say how.

Open-Meteo: weather

The forecast endpoint returned 552 hourly rows for a request of seven past days and sixteen forecast days, with no missing values in ten variables. It accepts up to sixteen days ahead (seventeen is refused with HTTP 400) and more than ninety days of past data. A request for Delhi was snapped to the grid point at 28.576° N, 77.187° E at 214 m elevation, and probing at 0.02° spacing shows the native cells are about 0.070° tall and 0.103° wide (8 × 10 km). Wind speed is returned in km/h unless metres per second are requested, and the application asks for metres per second throughout; conversions are recorded rather than silent.

The archive reaches back to 1940 and its newest hour was 23 hours old. Neither endpoint returns rate-limit headers, so the limit can only be seen by hitting it. Open-Meteo's published free allowance is 600 calls a minute, 5,000 an hour and 10,000 a day for non-commercial use; in practice, bursts of archive requests during the evaluation of the coupled model were refused and needed spacing and a retry. Licence: the data are CC BY 4.0, the free service is non-commercial, and commercial use needs a subscription.

The application also reads winds at 80, 120 and 180 m and on the 925, 850 and 700 hPa surfaces (about 0.8, 1.5 and 3.2 km above ground), which is what makes the vertical structure of the 3D flow real rather than decorative.

CAMS: the only current pollutant field

Open-Meteo serves CAMS global composition forecasts. A 92-day past window plus seven days ahead was returned with 1.5 % missing values; eight days ahead is refused. Units are µg/m³ (aerosol optical depth is dimensionless). Stepping 0.05° at a time across 1.1° of longitude gave only three distinct values, changing at 77.0° and 77.4°, so the effective cell is 0.4° or about 44 km and Delhi-NCR holds only about nine cells. This is why the live field on the Air Flow page is made of large blocks, and why no city-scale structure should be read into it.

OpenAQ: the observations

OpenAQ's version 3 API requires a key, allows 60 requests a minute (the response headers say so: limit 60, remaining counted down), and returned 115 reference-grade monitors inside the NCR box, 82 from CPCB, 30 from the CAAQM network and a handful from AirNow and the State Air programme. Each location lists its sensors with parameters and units.

The audit found three things worth knowing. Freshness is poor: the time since each location's last datum had a median of about 80 hours; one location was within three hours and 24 hours; 39 of 113 had not reported for more than a week, some for years. Licences are mostly unstated: one location listed a licence (US Public Domain); 114 listed none, so redistribution terms cannot be assumed and the application shows OpenAQ's attribution. Units are mixed: gases arrive as ppb, ppm or µg/m³, depending on the sensor, which is why every value is converted with a documented factor (25 °C, 1 atm) and keeps its original value and unit beside it.

OpenAQ also carries NO, NOx, temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and wind direction on CPCB stations. None of these is ingested yet. That corrects an earlier statement in this project that nitrogen oxide and station weather were unavailable: they are available, from a feed already in use.

The hourly-values response has value, a period with UTC and local start and end, a coverage block (expected and observed counts and percentage), and flagInfo. Periods are labelled as the provider gives them; for CPCB stations they end at the top of the Indian hour, which is why their UTC times fall on the half hour.

xKDR: the long history, and a six-hour error

The xKDR archive holds 196.5 million hourly rows from 558 stations since January 2009, 72 of them inside the NCR box, exported on 30 September 2026. It is a history, not a feed: CPCB stations in Delhi-NCR end on 1 September 2025, and only the US Embassy monitor continues, to 27 March 2026. Its metadata says timestamps are naive Indian Standard Time.

The audit tested that claim against the one station that both xKDR and OpenAQ serve over the same months. Over 1,306 overlapping hours the two series are identical (correlation 1.000, mean absolute difference 0.0 µg/m³), but only when xKDR's times are shifted by six hours relative to how the application had been reading them. A physical check agrees: read as IST, the Embassy series has its daily minimum at 10:00, when the afternoon boundary layer should make it 16:00 to 17:00; read as UTC it falls where it should. The other xKDR stations are correct as documented: across 40 CPCB stations the median daily minimum falls at 17:00 in the xKDR period and 16:00 in the OpenAQ period.

So the US Embassy rows are in UTC and the rest in IST. The ingest now reads that source as UTC, a regression test pins the behaviour, and the affected station (OpenAQ id 8118, the one the live forecast was served from) was re-ingested and cleaned: its daily minimum moved from 11:00 to 17:00. The previous raw rows are backed up. The trained model predates this fix, so retraining is recommended (it is a button on the Model Training page; nothing was retrained here).

Quality flags and the stored record

Every stored value keeps its original value and unit, the converted value, and a list of flags: low coverage, unit converted, unit suspect, out of range, flat-lined, sensor conflict, revised, and others. Values that fail a physical-plausibility check are kept and flagged, not clipped or dropped. Carbon monoxide is excluded entirely because every CO sensor in the window is labelled ppb with a median near 1, which is physically impossible; the labels, not the air, are wrong.

A single typed record now describes every value the application handles: its kind (observation, forecast, analysis, interpolation or simulation), the exact period it describes in UTC, when it was issued and retrieved, the station or the model cell and its size, original and standard units, flags, licence and attribution. The kind is part of a record's identity, so an interpolated value can never overwrite a measured one. The database section of the engineering report has the schema.

What this means in practice

For "what is the air like right now", the CAMS model is the only current source and is labelled as a model. For "what did the monitors read", the store is accurate but days old. For "what will this monitor read", the trained model needs fresh observations the feed does not currently supply, which is why it declines to forecast for the present. Closing these gaps does not need a new model: it needs a fresher observation feed (or direct CPCB access), the NO/NOx and station-weather parameters that OpenAQ already offers, and a retraining after the timestamp repair.

Which models, and why: an assessment before any new training

Candidates for station forecasting, regional prediction, weather and wind, transport and exposure, compared on what they need, what they give and what they cost. Only some were run; the report says which, and does not claim results for the rest. The recommendation is a layered system built on what already works, not a new large model.

The questions are practical: how pollutants affect air quality across the region, how weather and transport move them, where pollution is likely to rise next, and who is exposed. The rule for answering was to measure the cheap options first. Prediction intervals were fitted from validation residuals and cover 84 to 94 % of held-out values at a nominal 80 %, which is honest but slightly wide.

Three findings shaped everything else. First, persistence is a hard baseline in a source-poor model: air quality has strong memory, and any method that cannot add information beyond the last observation will lose to it. Second, raw CAMS is not a station forecaster here: it is biased and weakly correlated at 44 km cells. Third, the physical models (transport and coupled) are valuable for understanding movement and for visualisation, and are not forecasters, because they contain no emissions.

Why not FourCastNet, specifically

FourCastNet is a data-driven global weather emulator. It forecasts atmospheric variables such as wind at 0.25° (about 28 km) every six hours from an ERA5 starting state. For this project that is the wrong tool in three ways: Open-Meteo already serves hourly, 9 km forecasts from operational models at no cost; it has no pollutant channels, so it cannot say anything about concentration; and running it needs ERA5 initial conditions and a GPU. A justified weather use would be a regional ensemble of winds with quantified uncertainty, which is a research project, not a prerequisite. It is therefore not installed and its weights were not downloaded.

Recommended architecture

The system is five layers, each of which exists or can be built from what is already here.

1. Data layer. One typed, normalised record for every value (observation, forecast, analysis, interpolation, simulation), stored in PostgreSQL with PostGIS where available and SQLite for development. Provenance, units, periods, quality flags and licences travel with every row. This is implemented; the migration is 0002.

2. Observation and background fusion. Station observations interpolated between stations, with CAMS as the regional background and boundary condition. The interpolation's leave-one-out error is shown; CAMS is never presented as a measurement.

3. Station forecaster. Gradient-boosted models per pollutant and horizon, with CAMS and weather forecasts at the station as additional inputs, evaluated against persistence and climatology with the same time-ordered split. The one experiment worth running next is the CAMS-feature ablation: does adding CAMS reduce the 6 h and 24 h error? It is cheap and decides whether any heavier model is needed.

4. Movement and what-if. The transport and coupled models, labelled as simulations, for showing where the air goes and for scenario questions, with trajectories added for source attribution.

5. Exposure. Population-weighted concentration and a sensitive-site layer, produced from the station forecast and the interpolated field, once the population raster and site list are loaded.

How each model is evaluated

Every candidate is run on time-ordered splits with the training period strictly before the validation period, which is strictly before the test period; features are computed by the same function in training and serving, with a latency parameter, so a feature can never see the future. Scores are MAE, RMSE, R² and bias per pollutant and horizon, stratified by season and concentration band where the sample allows, always beside persistence and climatology. A candidate is adopted per cell only where it beats persistence on held-out data, and cells that do not are flagged in the interface. Intervals are checked for coverage. Spatial claims are tested leave-one-station-out. Simulations are scored against later observations at stations withheld from their starting field.

What would change the recommendation

A fresh, gap-free observation feed would make the station forecaster usable for the present and would let the physical models be started from measurements. An emissions inventory would turn the transport model into something that can forecast concentration changes rather than only carry them. A gridded observation product (satellite aerosol) would make a spatiotemporal network worth training. Until then the cheapest, most defensible improvements are the data repairs, the missing parameters, retraining on the corrected series, and the CAMS-feature ablation.

Database: schema and migration

The normalised schema is in docs/phase1/schema.postgis.sql and in Alembic revision 0002_environmental_schema (after 0001, which enables PostGIS). It has: env_source (provider, licence, attribution, native resolution, cadence); env_station and env_cell (point and polygon geometries with GiST indexes); env_record (one row per value, with kind constrained to observation, forecast, analysis, interpolation or simulation, a CHECK that forecasts carry an issue time and observations do not, and a CHECK that a record without a value is not usable); and the two exposure layers exposure_cell and sensitive_site, which are empty until a population raster and a facility list are loaded.

To apply it on a PostgreSQL server with PostGIS available: set DATABASE_URL in api/.env, then run .venv\Scripts\python.exe -m alembic upgrade head from api. The role needs permission to create the extension or PostGIS must already be installed. Rollback is alembic downgrade 0001. The development store keeps the same typed record in SQLite (app/envdata/schema.py, EnvStore), so code written against it runs unchanged against the PostgreSQL table.

Remaining limitations

The observation feed is days old, so the trained model refuses to forecast for the present and every "now" map is a replay or a model. The trained model was fitted before the timestamp repair and should be retrained (a user action). Raw CAMS is coarse (44 km) and, over Delhi stations, a worse predictor than yesterday's value. The coupled model has no emissions; its couplings did not reliably improve on plain transport and nothing beat persistence. The 3D concentration colouring inside the mixed layer assumes a uniform concentration with height, because there are no vertical measurements. Station licences in OpenAQ are mostly unstated, so redistribution terms are unknown. NO, NOx and station weather are available but not yet ingested. Population and sensitive-site layers are not loaded, so exposure is not computed. PostgreSQL and PostGIS are not configured here, so the migration is untested against a live server; its DDL is covered only by string-level tests. The model candidates marked as not evaluated in the model report were not run.
